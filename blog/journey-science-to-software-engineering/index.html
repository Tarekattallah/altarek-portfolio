My Journey from Science to Software Engineering

My transition from a scientific and pharmaceutical background into software development, and how analytical thinking, problem solving, and attention to detail became valuable skills in engineering.

Dec 08, 2025 • 4 min read
CareerSoftware EngineeringLearning

I did not start my career in software. I studied chemistry, earning a B.Sc. in Science (Special Chemistry) from Benha University, and my background is scientific and pharmaceutical: a world of lab work, precise procedures, and careful documentation. Moving into software engineering felt like a big jump at first. Looking back, I see that it was less a change of direction and more a change of tools. This is the story of that transition and what I carried with me.

Why Software?

What drew me to programming was the same thing that drew me to science: the satisfaction of understanding how something works and making it work better. In science, the feedback loop can take days or weeks. In software, I could form an idea, try it, and see the result in seconds. That speed of experimentation was addictive.

I also saw how much of the scientific and operational world depends on software, and how often that software is frustrating to use. I wanted to be on the side that builds the tools.

Starting from Fundamentals

My first structured step was a Diploma in Software Development at Benha University's Faculty of Science (2023 - 2024). It confirmed that this was the field I wanted to build a career in.

I did not want to learn a framework before learning how to think like a programmer, so I invested in fundamentals: Harvard's CS50x: Introduction to Computer Science, which took me from C to Python, algorithms, and data structures, alongside courses in Python, databases, JavaScript, and generative AI from Mahara Tech and Sprints. More importantly, these taught me how to break a problem down before writing any code.

From there I moved into web development: the Web Design training at the National Telecommunication Institute (NTI) through the Digital Egypt Youth Program, backend engineering with Node.js, and now the nine-month DigiLians AI-Powered Software Development Program, covering full-stack development, databases, cloud, DevOps, and security.

What Science Taught Me About Engineering

The Scientific Method Is Debugging

Debugging is just the scientific method applied to code. You observe a symptom, form a hypothesis about the cause, design an experiment to test it (a log line, a breakpoint, a minimal reproduction), and change one variable at a time. Developers who change five things at once and hope the bug disappears are skipping the method. Lab work trained me never to do that.

Precision and Attention to Detail

In a pharmaceutical context, a small error in a measurement or a label is not a minor issue. That habit of double-checking carried over directly: validating inputs, handling edge cases, and reading my own code before anyone else has to.

Documentation Is Part of the Work

A lab experiment that is not documented might as well not have happened. I bring the same mindset to software: clear README files, meaningful commit messages, and code that explains its intent. Writing this blog is partly an extension of that habit.

Process and Compliance

Regulated environments run on procedures, traceability, and audit trails. That background made enterprise concepts feel familiar: role-based access control, soft deletion to preserve history, audit logs, and business rules like expiry-based stock allocation all map to problems I already understood from the scientific side.

The Hard Parts

The transition was not smooth. The amount of material is overwhelming, and it is easy to feel behind people who started coding as teenagers. A few things helped me:

  • Building real projects early. Tutorials teach syntax; projects teach decisions. My understanding grew fastest when I built systems with real requirements.
  • Going deep on one stack. Instead of chasing every framework, I focused on JavaScript, Node.js, and databases until I could build a complete backend confidently.
  • Treating confusion as a normal state. In science, not knowing the answer yet is simply where research begins. The same is true in engineering.

Where I Am Now

Today I work as a full-stack developer with a strong focus on backend and enterprise systems: modular architectures, RBAC, data modeling, and business logic. I am now expanding into cloud, DevOps, and SRE, because I want to understand not just how software is built, but how it runs reliably in production.

Update, October 2026: Since writing this, I graduated from the DigiLians program, earned the AWS Certified Cloud Practitioner and AWS Certified Developer – Associate certifications, and completed Full Stack Development training at the Ministry of Justice, where I contributed to a judicial fee calculation platform built with React, TypeScript, Express, PostgreSQL, and Prisma.

Advice for Career Changers

  1. Your previous field is an asset, not a gap. Domain knowledge is something many developers lack.
  2. Learn the fundamentals before the frameworks. They outlast every trend.
  3. Build things that solve real problems, ideally from the domain you already know.
  4. Be patient with yourself. Consistency beats intensity.

I left the lab, but I did not leave science behind. Every bug I investigate is a small experiment, and that is one of my favorite parts of the job.
